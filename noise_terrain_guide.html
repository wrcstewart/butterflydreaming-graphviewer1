1. The one idea

There is no stored map. Height is a function.

height01(xKm, zKm)  →  a number in [0, 1]

It is a pure function of world position and seed: the same coordinates always return the same height, with nothing remembered between calls. Everything the page can do follows from that single property:

  • The world has no edge. Chunk (0,0) and chunk (10000, −7000) are the same amount of work. Walking away and back returns the identical ground, asserted by the self-test.
  • Chunks need no stitching. Two patches generated independently agree exactly where they meet, because both evaluate the same function at the same coordinates. The readout measures this at max difference 0, not "within a tolerance".
  • Detail can be added without moving anything. More octaves is more detail at the same place — which is a working level-of-detail path (§7).

The cost is that the function must be evaluated for every vertex drawn, every time. That is why the page is quick at 2×2 chunks and deliberate about going larger.

2. How the scene is constructed

Six steps, in order.

2.1 Chunks, generated independently

The page asks the terrain for k × k chunks, each chunkKm across, each sampled on a res × res grid — so (res+1)² heights per chunk, the extra row and column being the shared edge. The default 2×2 of 4 km at 64 cells is 8 km of ground in 16,900 samples.

Each chunk comes from its own chunk(cx, cz) call. That is deliberate: it is the method's central claim under test, so the page gets its geometry the same way a real streaming world would, rather than slicing up one big grid afterwards and proving nothing.

2.2 The edge test

Before drawing, every pair of adjacent chunks is compared along its shared boundary — k=2 gives 4 pairs, k=3 gives 12. The result appears on the shared edges line. It reads exactly 0 with every control engaged.

At k=1 there are no adjacent pairs, so the line says not tested rather than reporting a pass from a test that never ran.

2.3 One mesh per chunk

Each chunk becomes its own THREE.PlaneGeometry, placed so the set tiles a frame 2 units wide. Separate meshes, again deliberately: if the heights disagreed, a crack would open where they meet, and a single stitched surface would hide it.

2.4 Normals from an apron, not from the mesh

This is the one part that cannot be done per-chunk. computeVertexNormals() averages only the triangles of its own geometry, so a vertex on a tile boundary takes its normal from one side while its twin in the next tile takes a different one. The heights match perfectly and the shading still creases — it showed as two stitch lines dividing the scene into four.

So each chunk also carries a one-cell apron: the heights just outside its own edges. Normals come from the height gradient across that apron, so both tiles compute the same normal at a shared vertex — verified bit-identical. This is only possible because height01 is defined everywhere, which is the same property the whole method rests on.

2.5 Colour in a vertex attribute

The surface colour lives in a per-vertex attribute and the material stays white, because three.js multiplies material colour by vertex colour. Changing scheme refills the attribute and does not rebuild the field.

One consequence matters: a lit vertex colour is colour × (sun + hemi), and channels past 1.0 pin. At a gain of 2.55 a brown renders as 255,229,110 — pale cream — because red and green clip together and the result drifts yellow. The alpine scheme therefore runs a deliberately tame exposure (sun 0.75, hemi 0.40, gain 1.15). In the one scheme where colour carries information, exposure is set for colour fidelity, not for contrast.

2.6 The metre scale

The field is dimensionless, so one fixed constant fixes everything else: one height unit is 10,000 m. From that:

metre relief  = relief × mountain height × 10,000        (independent of km)
ground width  = chunks across × chunk size, in km
altitude      = valley floor + (h − lowest) × mountain height × 10,000

Because height has a scale of its own, the two controls separate and both do something physical: mountain height sets how tall the mountains are in metres, chunk size and count set how much ground is in frame — so widening the ground flattens the view, as looking at more land does. The surface is drawn true to scale between them, so an on-screen grade is a real grade.

3. How the noise is generated

height01 runs the following in this order. Each stage is a control, so this is also the map of what affects what.

  (x, z) in km
      │
  1.  warp ............ displace the sample point by a second, slower noise field
      │
  2.  stretch ......... divide x and z unequally  (area-preserving)
      │
  3.  octave sum ...... the mountains: 7 rotated simplex samples at doubling
      │                 frequency and 2^−H amplitude, each weighted by w
      │
  4.  ÷ norm .......... normalise to roughly [−1, 1]
      │
  5.  slow layer ...... a 2-octave swell, sampled at the UNWARPED position
      │
  6.  combine ......... baseShare·swell + (1−baseShare)·beltMask(swell)·mountains
      │
  7.  squash .......... hard clamp, or a soft knee, into [0, 1]
      ▼
  height in [0, 1]

3.1 The octave sum — the mountains

sum = Σ  aₒ · wₒ · simplex( rotateₒ(x/su, z/sv) · fₒ + offsetₒ )
       o
      fₒ = (1/widest) · 2ᵒ          amplitude  aₒ = (2^−H)ᵒ
  • f₀ = 1/widest, with coordinates in kilometres. So the lowest octave's wavelength is the widest feature, and the finest detail is widest / 2^(octaves−1). What governs the look is the ratio of widest to the ground in view: 4 km features across 8 km of ground gives two big features per side.
  • Each octave is rotated by 0.83 radians and offset by (17.3o, −9.1o). Simplex noise has a lattice, and both the rotation and the offsets exist to stop the octaves lining up on it. The readout's anisotropy figure is the check that this worked; it reads about 0.9, i.e. no detectable direction.
  • gain = 2^−H is what makes the sum fractional Brownian motion. Note the H asked for is not the H delivered — see §6.
  • norm = Σ gainᵒ, so the sum lands in roughly [−1, 1] whatever the octave count. With stable zoom on, that divisor is computed over a fixed budget instead, which is what makes adding octaves leave the coarse shape exactly where it was.

3.2 The four modifiers

Plain octave noise is statistically homogeneous — the same everywhere — so every patch is equally rough and nothing distinguishes a plain from a range. These break that, and they break different things:

what it breakshow
warpuniformity of placementthe sample point is displaced by a slower noise field before the octaves are summed, so ridges become sinuous and peaks stop being evenly scattered
stretchisotropyx and z are scaled unequally, elongating every feature along one axis. Area-preserving — √r one way, 1/√r the other — because stretching one axis alone just lowers the frequency along it and smooths the whole surface
heterouniformity of roughnesseach octave's weight w is modulated by the accumulated elevation so far, so detail appears where the ground is already high and dies in the lows: rough peaks over smooth plains
the slow layerthe scaleadds a swell much larger than any mountain, which also decides where mountains grow at all

hetero's weight is normalised to average exactly 1 (w = (1−h+h·elev)/(1−h/2)), so it redistributes detail rather than removing it. Without that divisor the mean weight is 1−h/2 and the control simply smoothed the terrain.

All the figures quoted for these three are measured against plain noise with the slow layer OFF, and that matters, because the belt overlaps with hetero. The honest comparison is the correlation between a patch's roughness and its height — which is what "rough peaks over smooth plains" actually means:

roughness-to-height correlation
plain noise0.008 — none at all, every patch equally rough
hetero 1, no belt0.439
the belt, hetero 00.793
the belt and hetero 10.831

The belt is much the stronger mechanism, because mountains only grow on the swell's high ground, which ties roughness to height by construction. So at the page's defaults hetero adds little — reach for it when you want rough peaks without confining the mountains to a belt, which is a different landscape: mountains everywhere, but only the high ones rugged.

3.2a Which mechanism to reach for

They are not interchangeable, and two of them cost something:

  • Want large-scale organisation — ranges here, plains there? The slow layer. Nothing else supplies structure bigger than one mountain.
  • Want lineation, ridge-and-valley country? Stretch. It is the only one that produces a direction, and it costs roughness (3.55 → 2.33 at 3:1) because a stretched axis has gentler gradients.
  • Want irregular placement, sinuous ridges, uneven spacing? Warp. It costs the grain if you also want stretch.
  • Want rough summits over smooth ground, with no belt? Hetero. Free of side effects — it keeps isotropy and does not reduce roughness.

3.3 The slow layer, and why it is one field doing two jobs

height = baseShare·swell  +  (1 − baseShare)·beltMask(swell)·mountains

The swell is two octaves of its own noise at wavelength baseKm, sampled at the unwarped, unstretched position so the regional shape is not dragged about by controls meant for the mountains.

It is used twice: as the gentle rolling ground and as the mask deciding where mountains grow. So ranges stand on the high ground of the swell, its flanks carry low-amplitude mountains — which is what a foothill is — and it keeps rolling across the lows where no mountains grow at all. That also gets the geology right for nothing: real ranges sit on uplifted ground rather than scattered at random.

The mask is a smoothstep: u = (swell01 − (1−coverage))/foothills + 0.5, clamped, then u²(3−2u). Smoothstep because it is flat at both ends, so the belt has no crease at its edge.

3.4 The squash

0.5 + 0.85·combined is finally forced into [0, 1]. The original method uses a hard clamp, and where a broad swell overshoots it the whole top is sheared off into a dead-flat mesa — measured, 5.3% of the grid as a single region in one corner at widest 16 km over 3 octaves. Soft ceiling replaces the corner with a knee over the outer 12%, approaching the rail asymptotically and never reaching zero gradient, so a swell stays curved however far it overshoots.

6. Things that look like faults and are not

  • The H asked for is not the H delivered. 0.50 gives 0.47, but 0.90 gives only 0.72. Discrete octave spacing and simplex's own spectral shape are not a true power law. This is the method's behaviour, and the readout shows both figures rather than implying they agree.
  • Relief is always exactly 1.000 with the layer off. height01 clamps to [0, 1], so the method normalises itself and relief is not a measure here. That is why metres come from the height control alone.
  • The anisotropy figure is noisy, ±0.4 on one reading at this grid size, because a red spectrum puts nearly all its power where the grid has fewest samples per direction.
  • Gentle ground outside a belt is the design, not the clamp. The readout distinguishes them.
  • Stretch costs some roughness. A stretched axis has gentler gradients; even area-preserving, roughness falls from 3.55 to 2.33 at 3:1. Unavoidable.

7. The zoom ceiling, and the LOD path

Zooming in past the finest octave shows nothing new. The octave set is fixed, so detail stops at widest / 2^(octaves−1) — 62.5 m at the defaults. Sampling one 200 m patch from 28.6 m spacing down to 0.8 m gives relief 0.1975, 0.1976, 0.1975: a smoother interpolation of the same surface, no new ground. Raising cells per chunk does not help; raising octaves does.

That is also the level-of-detail path, and it works because height01 is a pure function: adding octaves while zooming leaves the coarse shape within half a percent, and exactly with stable zoom on. Far chunks few octaves, near chunks many, and the mountain does not move. Nothing in the page consumes this yet — a walk-through would.
