Method C: height is a pure function of world position and seed, so chunks generated independently agree exactly where they meet. The world is endless, not a tile.

Sets the octave gain to 2−H. The H asked for is not the H delivered — measured, 0.50 gives 0.47 but 0.90 gives only 0.72, because discrete octaves and simplex's own spectral shape are not a true power law. The readout shows both.
The lowest octave's wavelength. The finest detail is this divided by 2octaves−1; the readout gives it in metres.
break up the uniformity
Plain octave noise is statistically homogeneous — the same everywhere, so every patch is equally rough and there are no plains against ranges. Measured as the spread of local relief. These three raise it, and they break different things. All with every chunk edge still exact. The readout compares against the same settings with these off, because the absolute figure depends on the grid and window too.
Elongates every feature along one axis: ridge-and-valley country instead of a field of lumps. This one makes the terrain genuinely anisotropic on purpose, so the readout will report a direction — that is the control working, and the only place in this work where a direction is wanted.
Area-preserving, stretching one axis by √r and squeezing the other by the same, because stretching one alone just lowers the frequency along it and smooths the whole surface: roughness fell 3.55 to 1.49 at 3:1 that way, against 2.33 keeping the area. Some loss is unavoidable — a stretched axis has gentler gradients.
Displaces the sample point by a slower second noise field, so ridges become sinuous and peaks stop being evenly scattered. Raises the uniformity spread by about a quarter. It nudges the direction reading to about 1.4 on average, which stays under the 1.6 detection threshold.
Warp fights stretch. Measured over four seeds, stretch 3:1 alone gives a grain of 2.15 and keeps it under hetero (2.01), but warp at 1.5 km bends the ground enough to randomise the direction and the grain drops to 1.43 — gone. At 0.5 km it survives at 1.93. Keep warp under about 0.5 km if you want grain; use it freely if you do not.
The direct answer to uniform peaks. Each octave's amplitude is modulated by the accumulated elevation, so detail appears where the ground is already high and dies in the lows — rough peaks over smooth plains. Musgrave's multifractal idea. Stays isotropic (0.89), unlike the other two.
It bites above about 0.75: +2% at 0.5, +13% at 0.75, +33% at 1.0, because the weight only swings the full 0–2 at the top of the range. The first version of this control instead smoothed the terrain — the weight averaged 1 − h/2, so it deleted detail rather than moving it. It now averages exactly 1, and the readout reports absolute roughness so a repeat would show.
Divides by a fixed octave budget rather than by the octaves actually summed, so adding octaves to zoom in leaves the coarse shape exactly where it was instead of shifting it by about half a percent. Off by default only because it changes the amplitude by 2.6% and the baseline here has to stay the document's own.
chunks
Each chunk is drawn as its OWN MESH, built by an independent call, so any disagreement at a shared edge would open a visible crack. The readout measures every shared edge as well.
Walk the world. Nothing is stored, so there is no edge to the map and no limit to how far this goes — try a few thousand chunks out.
view
Sets the relief in metres; the readout gives it. height01 already returns [0, 1], so unlike the spectral page the raw relief here is fixed at 1.000 by the method itself — it clamps. That is the method's doing, not a normalisation added here, and it is why metres come from this control alone.
Chunk size and chunk count set the ground width, so adding chunks flattens the view — the same mountains across more land.
A low sun is the lever for shallow relief: shading sensitivity goes as grade × cot(elevation).
